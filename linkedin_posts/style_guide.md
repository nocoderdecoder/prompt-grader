# LinkedIn Style Guide — Anshul Gupta

## Length
220-280 words per post. Hard limit. Count words. Cut if over 280.

## Structure
- Short paragraphs, 2-4 sentences each
- One central idea, developed linearly
- Ending that reframes or lands somewhere earned
- No headers or subheadings inside the post
- Bullet lists only when the content genuinely requires them

## Openings — use one of these three only
1. Sharp thesis: "The bottleneck in tech is moving from building to distribution."
2. Thought experiment: "Say you assigned the same task to two people."
3. Slightly contrarian: "Pretty basic, but a lot of people haven't done this yet."

Never: hook-bait, dramatic rhetorical questions, "Unpopular opinion:"

## Argument arc
Acknowledge something real or popular → introduce the complication → land on a sharper conclusion. Give the popular thing its due first. The counter-point must feel earned.

## Voice — most important
Write as a smart professional sharing a personal, honest observation. NOT as an analyst or consultant addressing the market. Ask yourself: does this sound like a person thinking something through, or a McKinsey slide deck? If the latter, rewrite it.

## Tone
- Calm, measured, confident. Not excitable.
- Honest and direct. Willing to say: "I don't think so." "That hasn't changed."
- Not preachy. No lecturing.
- Grounded in what actually matters in practice.

## Hard avoids
- No emojis
- No hashtags (or 1-2 max if they genuinely fit — usually none)
- Never write: "here's why this matters", "most people get this wrong", "game-changer", "this is huge", "in today's rapidly evolving landscape", "it's important to note"
- NO statistics, percentages, or data points of any kind — not even approximate ones. Replace any urge to cite a number with a concrete observation instead.
- NEVER use the words "enterprises" or "organizations" — use: people, teams, most companies, or direct address
- NO specific model version numbers unless verified via web search right now. Hallucinated versions (e.g. made-up model names) destroy credibility.
- No personal anecdotes
- No analyst framing: "vendor strategy", "procurement strategy", "deployment velocity"
- No AI filler phrases

## Topics Anshul writes about
- Specific AI tools with honest, practical takes
- The human element AI doesn't replace: judgment, taste, critical thinking, fundamentals
- Business and strategy angles on tech trends: distribution, GTM, what actually wins
- Counter-intuitive angles on overhyped topics
- How to use AI as a thinking partner, not just an output machine

## Endings
Forward-looking, quiet authority. Reframes the post in one or two sentences.
Pattern: "The people who win won't be the ones who [obvious thing]. They'll be the ones who [less obvious thing]."

## Language
- Plain English
- Specific when needed: tool names, workflow descriptions
- Short sentences for emphasis
- Contrast works well: "The first kind... The second kind..."
- No em-dashes

### Post A (225 words)
The bottleneck in tech is moving from building to distribution.

For a long time, building was the hard part. You needed a team, time, and real resources. Most ideas never made it past that stage, not because they were bad but because execution was genuinely difficult.

AI changed that. A non technical person can now build a functional, presentation ready product in days. What used to take months is now accessible to almost anyone.

But that means the market is getting flooded. New products every week, solving similar problems, all reasonably well built.

So the competition has shifted. Who is closest to the customer? Who already has a follower base? Who can stay relevant not for weeks but for months? What is the channel strategy?

Distribution was always an important part of the equation. It is just going to keep getting more important from here.

### Post B (248 words)
Say you assigned the same task to two people.

Both have access to the same AI tools, same models, same internet. And you tell them: use AI as much as you want, for as long as you want. No restrictions whatsoever.

Will the quality of the output be the same?

I don't think so.

Yes, AI is an equaliser. It gives access to knowledge you don't have, skills you never learned, and drafts you couldn't have produced alone. That part is real.

But then comes the part AI can't do for you.

Reading the output and sensing something is off. Asking a sharper follow-up instead of accepting the first draft, even if it looks polished. Knowing how to combine multiple outputs into one piece that actually flows.

That's not a skill AI gives you. That's judgment. And judgment is built over years of doing the work manually, sitting with tough feedback, figuring out what actually lands, and developing a feel for what good looks like.

The best outputs come from critical thinking, sharp prompting, and a human who knows what they're looking for and won't settle until they find it. AI just makes that process faster for the people who already know how to think.

### Post C (279 words)
Vibe coding is probably the best thing that happened to people who stopped coding, or never really could in the first place. But there's one honest conversation missing from most posts about it.

At the beginning, it genuinely feels magical. You type one prompt and something real appears on your screen. You feel faster than ever, more capable than ever, like the barrier between idea and product has finally dropped.

Then you try to change one small thing and the illusion cracks. A simple heading update breaks something unrelated. You add login and suddenly parts of the app behave like they came from an older version. You spend an hour fixing what should have taken five minutes.

This isn't a flaw in the AI. It's a process problem.

What vibe coding really did was make it incredibly easy to jump straight to output. So people skip the part where you think through the system. You stop thinking about structure and start thinking about fixes. You stop designing flows and start reacting to whatever breaks next. The AI keeps generating, but the foundation underneath keeps getting shakier.

Good software has always required clarity on how pieces connect, what depends on what, and what should stay stable over time. That hasn't changed.

The only difference now is that you can build something that looks finished long before it's actually thought through.

Vibe coding isn't replacing fundamentals. It's just speeding up the consequences of ignoring them. The people who win with it won't be the ones who prompt the fastest. They'll be the ones who still think like builders before they start generating.
